# A mortal's guide to Tempting Fates

The gods still receive their offerings. The granaries are less reliable.

You are a mortal in an original Greek-myth archipelago where the institutions that once kept people fed, sheltered and remembered are coming apart. Priests disagree. Harbor officials charge for repairs that never arrive. Families keep promises the city has forgotten. A divine name can be an object of devotion, a political excuse, or both.

You are not required to restore the world. Decide what your traveler wants, whom they care about, what work they know, and what they would rather not become. The game follows what you do with those answers.

## What you need

One referee and one to six players; a d4, d6, d8, d10, d12 and d20 (or an agreed dice tool); a copy of the character sheet; and a way to record possessions, time and discoveries. Start with the [rules](RULES.md), then use [Quickstart](QUICKSTART.md). Solo means one player with a referee or an appropriately configured ARGO host—not an all-knowing player reading the referee's secrets.

The campaign uses a compact adaptation of Knave first edition. There are no mandatory classes. Equipment and choices matter more than a profession label. The campaign book supports human-refereed play now; its accompanying software calculates bounded rules results and seeds ARGO's existing world. Fully automated combat, travel and faction transactions require a later authority extension.

## Make a traveler

Use the complete procedure in RULES.md. Then answer five ordinary questions:

1. What do you do when nobody is having an adventure?
2. What brought you to the harbor?
3. Who here might recognize you, and what did they actually see?
4. What do you need in the next few days?
5. What principle or habit tends to win when you are tired?

A ferryman, a mason, a household attendant, a healer's assistant, a temple copyist and an exile can all travel together. These are backgrounds, not permission to override the rules or another character's will. A background establishes plausible familiarity; the referee still describes what information is available and whether a particular action needs a check.

Write a short nature and one or two priorities. “Slow to trust promises; protects anyone doing difficult work” is useful. A numerical alignment or a universal approval score is unnecessary.

Other people hold their own impressions of you. A potter might remember that you returned a borrowed lamp; a rival might remember that you returned it late. Neither impression lives on your character sheet as what everyone thinks. People can disagree, miss events, hear an inaccurate account or change their minds.

## The table loop

The referee describes what your traveler can perceive. You say what you want and how you try to achieve it. The referee clarifies uncertainty, time and foreseeable stakes. Roll only when the outcome is uncertain and failure would matter. Resolve the stated consequences, record what actually changed, then let the next person act.

“I inspect the hinge before touching the door” is an action. “I roll Perception” is a request for a procedure: say what you are looking at and how. Obvious features do not require a roll. A good plan may remove uncertainty; it does not require a bonus to remain a good plan.

Ask what a risky action could cost before committing. The referee can withhold a secret while still explaining what your traveler would recognize as dangerous. A failed check should change the situation rather than create an endless sequence of identical attempts.

## What Greek myth changes

| Concern | What it means in play |
| --- | --- |
| Hospitality | A roof, a meal, a guest's conduct and a host's obligations belong to particular people. Breaking them creates witnesses and consequences, not an automatic universal curse score. |
| Oaths | Record the actual words, parties and conditions. An oath someone imagined is not one you made. Divine witnesses are not a substitute for the recipient's understanding. |
| Burial and remembrance | The dead leave possessions, claims and people behind. A funeral can concern transport, money, testimony and family conflict before it concerns a monster. |
| Patronage | A sanctuary may offer shelter, work, training or a ritual under stated terms. Devotion does not give its officials ownership of a mortal's choices. |
| Craft and scarcity | A bronze fitting, a sound boat or a winter store can matter more than a famous sword. Tools have owners and materials come from somewhere. |
| Glory | Stories spread through actual tellers. Reputation can be contradictory, local and wrong. A widely told story is still a claim. |
| Mortality | Extra years, if possible, are not youth or fulfillment. Another person's life is never an automatic resource on your sheet. |

Myths in this campaign are an original continuity, not a claim that ancient Greek traditions agreed about every god and event. People within the world may tell different versions.

## Exploration and leaving

Choose a destination, agree on supplies, and tell the referee what you hope to learn. Roads, crossings and boats have stated costs. A session need not end in a fight or at a quest objective. Returning to the harbor, taking work, changing direction and staying home are valid decisions.

For a short solo visit, pursue one small intention and stop at a safe checkpoint. For a party expedition, invite each traveler to describe their preparations; decide who carries what, how you communicate, and when you turn back. A player who is absent has not agreed to donate, spend, fight or reveal a secret.

Information learned alone stays with the people who acquired it. Telling the party creates a new disclosure, with a teller and a time. Joining a party does not publish your private history.

## The Fates

Clotho concerns beginnings. Lachesis concerns measure. Atropos concerns endings. They are not a quest board, a shop or a three-person court of moral worth. Asking the wrong sister a question may earn a short, perfectly accurate answer that is useless to your purpose.

There are stories of things that alter the measure of a life. A story does not prove an object exists in your world. If you seek such a thing, its location depends on what actually happened to it. A guide from another world is an account, not a command that makes a relic appear.

If an honest gift of time becomes possible, its donor must understand and freely choose its specific cost. Persuasion, an oath, a failed saving throw, a priest's permission or a narrator's flourish cannot supply that choice. You may refuse the gift or stop seeking it. The world does not owe you this encounter or an explanation of everything beyond it.

## The narrator

ARGO's narrator watches from outside the world: tired, dry and occasionally sarcastic. The traveler cannot bargain with him, summon him or treat an aside as an NPC speaking. His prose cannot award an item, declare someone else's consent or change an already recorded action.

*Another expedition. The harbor has survived several. It keeps the receipts.*
